## They say

Guillermo O'Donnell's "Delegative Democracy" (1994) named a familiar pattern: free elections, then a president who governs as if the mandate were a blank check — weak horizontal accountability, little patience for institutions, a direct bond with "the people." It is democracy without the full republican furniture. A reader of comparative politics expects this plate next to Linz.

## Conciliatorics' view

The treatise uses the presidential term as a structure already in the world: even a popular holder is blocked from remaining. That is conciliation as ended function. O'Donnell's delegative pattern is the opposite reading of the same office: the term as a license to unify until the next election, or beyond.

Linz feared dual legitimacy and rigidity. O'Donnell feared a winner who treats institutions as optional. Conciliatorics can hold both without making presidentialism a fate.

## Where they meet

Both care whether an ended mandate is real. Both refuse "there was an election" as the whole of function. Madison's ambition, Linz's perils, O'Donnell's delegation, and Mixed Synthesis belong at one table for readers of presidential systems.

## Where they part

O'Donnell is classifying actually existing democracies in a historical moment, with a Latin American archive. Conciliatorics is not that archive. Delegative democracy is not Unification Synthesis, even when they coincide. The handshake is the blank-check term. The jobs remain different.
