## They say

Arend Lijphart's consociational democracy — grand coalition, mutual veto, proportionality, segmental autonomy — is a design for plural societies that will not first become one people. *Democracy in Plural Societies* (1977) and *Patterns of Democracy* (1999) are the books a comparativist will know. The claim is practical: Westminster majority rule can be a weapon in a deeply divided place. Sharing power without asking groups to dissolve is a way to keep the peace.

## Conciliatorics' view

Conciliation Synthesis stays functional by accepting that a form will end, and that no one part is the whole. Lijphart's mix is nearby as a constitutional fact. The Parcels Council also refuses fusion: full harmony is not required; no parcel is to be invalidated.

EIC does not take up segmental cleavage as a variable, and it does not rewrite the mutual veto as an operator. The contact is sharing without merging, not a recipe for cabinets.

## Where they meet

Both refuse "one people first, then government." Aristotle's polity, Rawls's overlapping consensus, Mouffe's agonism, and Lijphart's consociation are not one theory. They are a family of mixings a reader of divided polities expects to find.

## Where they part

Lijphart is designing democratic institutions for plural societies, with a record of cases. Conciliatorics is not a handbook for those cases. A consociational settlement can still unify — a durable cartel of elites that will not be revised. It can also conciliate. "This elite cartel has lasted" is not yet function. The handshake is sharing without fusion. The jobs remain different.
