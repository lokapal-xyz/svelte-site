## They say

Montesquieu's *Spirit of the Laws* (1748) tied laws to climate, manners, commerce, and the distribution of powers. The separation of powers is the portable piece Madison took. The larger claim is that a form has to fit what it can bear — a republic, a monarchy, a despotism each have a principle, and the principle can be corrupted. A reader who has only "checks and balances" already has the spirit: laws are not a kit.

## Conciliatorics' view

Resolution's viability is what a context can actually bear. Mixed Synthesis names two ways a mixed case holds. Montesquieu's fit is nearby as a constitutional sociology: the form and the manners have to be able to live together.

EIC does not take up climate as a cause, and it does not rewrite the three powers as operators. The contact is the fit, not the geography.

## Where they meet

Both refuse a constitution as a gift that can be dropped on any people. Madison's ambition, Aristotle's polity, Tocqueville's mores, and Montesquieu's spirit can sit together. A reader of government systems expects this plate behind Madison.

## Where they part

Montesquieu is writing an eighteenth-century science of laws, with a typology and a fear of despotism. Conciliatorics is not that science. "Principle of government" is not a status dichotomy. Viability is not climate. The handshake is the fit. The jobs remain different.
