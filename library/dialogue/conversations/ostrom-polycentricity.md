## They say

Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) is the book that made it possible to refuse a forced choice: either a sovereign must own the resource, or the market must parcel it into private holdings, or the commons will fail. Studying irrigation, forests, fisheries, and pastures, she showed that the people who use a shared resource can, under some conditions, craft and enforce their own rules. The design principles are well known even to readers who have not memorized the list: clear boundaries, fit between rules and local conditions, collective-choice procedures, monitoring, graduated sanctions, ways of settling disputes, recognition of the users' right to organize, and — for larger resources — nested enterprises.

Polycentricity is the wider name. Many centers of decision, overlapping, none of them the only center. *Understanding Institutional Diversity* (2005) treats that craft as ordinary institutional work, not as a miracle.

## Conciliatorics' view

Status Reciprocity pairs an interstitial choice with an intrinsic face. Unification leads to centralization: lasting by gathering authority, even at the cost of function. Conciliation leads to decentralization: staying functional by rotating, splitting, or replacing parts. The questions become usable. What blocks decentralization here? What does each intrinsic status cost? Which frameworks would actually help a turn, rather than decorate it?

The Conciliation Pathway is how that turn is lived: iterated imperdurable functionality. A release that is allowed to end, so the next release can work. Nested enterprises, in Ostrom's sense, are one way a larger function can continue while particular rule-sets are revised.

## Where they meet

A working commons does not have to gather all authority into one lasting center. Users who monitor one another, who can change a rule without pretending the last rule was eternal, are already near conciliation even if they have never heard the word. A reader who comes from institutionalism will find the handshake here, not a quarrel.

## Where they part

Ostrom is writing institutional design for commons — water, woods, fish, pasture — and for the public economies that resemble them. Conciliatorics is not a substitute handbook for those cases. Government systems, local organizations, and protocols are live surfaces for Status Reciprocity; irrigation districts are not, by that fact, EIC's job. The shared problem is how a mixed case stays functional without pretending one form is eternal. The field's own vocabulary already says a great deal about that. A term earns its place here only where the interstitial reading still adds a question: not only "which rules work," but "is this lasting by unification, or working by conciliation?"
