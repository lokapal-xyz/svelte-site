## They say

Francis Fukuyama's *End of History and the Last Man* (1992) argued that liberal democracy had won the ideological contest: not that events would stop, but that a viable alternative form of government had run out. Recognition, thymos, the last man — the Hegelian furniture was part of the claim. A reader who has only the title already has the hope the Incompatibility Axiom names: a last <plain>arrangement</plain> that works and lasts.

## Conciliatorics' view

The Incompatibility Axiom says functionality and perdurability cannot coexist in a mixed physiology. Nations proclaim eternal inevitability; empires fall. Fukuyama's last form is the late-twentieth-century face of that proclamation, in a liberal key.

Mixed Synthesis still has two states. A liberal democracy can conciliate — terms end, governments fall, the function continues. It can also unify — treat itself as the last form, and treat revision as betrayal. The end of history is a unification of the horizon.

## Where they meet

Both take ideology and recognition seriously as political facts, not as froth. Huntington's degree of government, Fukuyama's last form, and Mixed Synthesis are not one measurement. A reader expects this plate next to Huntington.

## Where they part

Fukuyama is writing a world-historical claim about ideological evolution. Conciliatorics is not a reply to 1989, and it does not take up thymos. "Liberal democracy" is not Conciliation Synthesis. The handshake is the last form. The jobs remain different. The axiom does not need to win a debate with Hegel; it needs to stay testable against mixed cases.
