## They say

Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* (2012) split extractive from inclusive institutions. Extractive institutions gather wealth and power for a few; inclusive ones spread incentives and political voice. The portable claim is that institutions, not geography or culture as destiny, explain divergence. A reader who has only that split already has a development story: gathering versus spreading.

## Conciliatorics' view

Unification lasts by gathering authority; conciliation stays functional by rotating, splitting, or replacing parts. Status Reciprocity reads those as centralization and decentralization in ordinary names. Extractive/inclusive is nearby as an institutional fact. It is not a translation.

Inclusive institutions can still seek to last forever. Extractive ones can be unstable. The physiological question — functionality versus perdurability in a mixed case — is not the same measurement as "inclusive enough to grow."

## Where they meet

Both refuse geography-as-fate and culture-as-fate as the whole story. Both treat the rules of the game as load-bearing. North's institutions, Ostrom's polycentricity, and extractive/inclusive can sit with unification/conciliation as a family of cuts. A buffet of this kind without this plate would be noticed.

## Where they part

Acemoglu and Robinson are writing a popular political economy of development, with a strong causal bet on institutions. Conciliatorics is not that book with extra notation. Inclusive is not Conciliation. Extractive is not Unification, even when they coincide. The handshake is gathering versus spreading. The jobs remain different.
