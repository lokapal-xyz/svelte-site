## They say

Robert Michels's *Political Parties* (1911) watched organizations that began as instruments of the many produce oligarchies of officials. The iron law is the portable sentence: organization implies oligarchy. Expertise, the need to decide, the apathy of the many, the self-interest of leaders — the mechanism is sociological, not a smear. A reader who has only the iron law already has the reversal: the means of emancipation becoming a new elite.

## Conciliatorics' view

Inventive Ossification is a reversal in which the edification, or the organization that carries it, becomes the end. Michels is nearby as a party fact. Schumpeter's competitive elitism will take the next step: make the elite contest the method. Conciliatorics does not have to follow. It can name the reversal without making oligarchy a law of all mixed cases.

## Where they meet

Both describe a tool of many-member action that starts to run for itself. Weber's bureaucracy, Michels's party, and an ossified parcel rhyme. A reader of government systems expects this plate next to Weber.

## Where they part

Michels claimed a law. Conciliatorics claims a risk that has to be conciliated, not a fate. Ostrom's users who monitor one another, Tocqueville's associations, and iterated terms are not refutations of Michels in his own terms. They are reminders that "organization implies oligarchy" is already a unification of the problem. The handshake is the reversal inside the organization. The jobs remain different.
