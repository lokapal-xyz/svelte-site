## They say

Robert Dahl's *Polyarchy* (1971) gave comparative politics a usable pair of dimensions: public contestation and participation. Democracy, in the ideal sense — a regime fully responsive to all its citizens — is not what we have. What we can score, more honestly, is polyarchy: highly inclusive, extensively open to opposition. The institutional list many readers will already know (elected officials, free and fair elections, inclusive suffrage, the right to run, freedom of expression, alternative information, associational autonomy) is a way of asking *how democratic* an actually existing regime is, without pretending the ideal has arrived.

Juan Linz asked a different, equally famous question. In "The Perils of Presidentialism" (1990) he argued that, on balance, parliamentary democracies have been more conducive to stable democracy than presidential ones — especially where cleavages are deep and parties are many. Presidentialism, as he draws it, has a dual legitimacy (president and legislature, both elected), a rigid term that is hard to interrupt without a crisis, and a winner-take-all logic that can turn opponents into enemies. The United States is the long exception, not the template. Linz's wider work on regime types — democratic, authoritarian, totalitarian — is the other half of the same craft: classifying what a regime *is*, and how it fails.

## Conciliatorics' view

Mixed Synthesis does not score contestation, and it does not choose parliament against the presidency. It names two interstitial states of a mixed case. Unification Synthesis lasts by gathering authority, even at the cost of function — the empire that remains one. Conciliation Synthesis stays functional by accepting that a given form will end — the presidential term used in the treatise as a structure already in the world, not as a vote for presidentialism.

The Incompatibility Axiom is the claim underneath: in a mixed physiology, functionality and perdurability cannot coexist. "How democratic is this?" and "is this unifying or conciliating?" are related questions. They are not the same measurement. A polyarchy can still unify. An authoritarian regime is often unifying in the ordinary sense; that does not exhaust what the axiom is about.

## Where they meet

Both Dahl and Linz care whether representation actually works, and both refuse the romance of a ruler who never leaves. Dual legitimacy, inclusive contestation, and an ended term are all ways of not letting one will become the whole. A reader who already uses polyarchy as a yardstick, or Linz as a warning about constitutional form, is asking questions Conciliatorics also has to ask once government systems are the applied niche.

## Where they part

Dahl measures inclusion and contestation. Linz types constitutional forms and their failure modes. Conciliatorics asks a physiological question. That difference bites on the presidential term itself. Linz treats the rigid term as one of presidentialism's perils: you cannot easily replace a failed president without a crisis. The treatise treats the fact that the term *ends* as the health of a mixed case. Those claims can both be true in their own vocabularies. The example is not a defense of presidentialism as a regime type, and it is not a dismissal of Linz. It is a reading of what accepting an end does for function — a reading that would still apply to a parliamentary cabinet that falls, a protocol that versions, or a custom a generation is allowed to revise.
