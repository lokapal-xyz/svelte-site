## They say

Early Buddhist teaching names three marks of conditioned things. They are usually met as anicca, dukkha, and anattā: impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self. The *Anattalakkhana Sutta* is a well-known scene for the last of these; anicca runs through the Nikāyas as the first. Whatever is taken up as lasting — a body, a feeling, a view, a world — does not stay. Clinging to what passes is bound up with dukkha. The teaching is a diagnosis of experience, and a path, not a theory of states or firms.

Walpola Rahula's *What the Buddha Taught* (1959) is one of the books that carried this cut into a wider English-speaking readership. The popular version is already enough for the contact: things do not last, and taking them as if they did is the wound.

## Conciliatorics' view

Imperdurability, in Attributes Status, is the desirable pole of a dichotomy, paired with functionality. Perdurability is the detrimental one. That pairing runs against the instinct to last. Mixed physiology inherits the instinct anyway: a perdurability longing alongside a functionality longing. The Incompatibility Axiom is what follows. The Conciliation Pathway is how a mixed case can still work — by iterating a function that consents to end.

The treatise is blunt about the attachment. We look for what lasts in <plain>order</plain> to compensate for a finite and absurd existence. To be replaced feels like meaninglessness; to be forgotten, like an empty legacy. The intellect can grant the brevity of an insect. It still cannot let go of this particular stay. *I must endure.*

## Where they meet

Refusing to let a form last is not nihilism. It can be the condition of a healthier larger function — or of not taking the passing as if it were a possession. A reader who already grants anicca will not find Conciliatorics' lean toward imperdurability exotic. Both descriptions treat clinging to a form as the beginning of a worse stay, not as loyalty.

## Where they part

Early Buddhist teaching is a path of liberation from dukkha. It does not need empires and presidential terms. Conciliatorics does, because every development in EIC has to remain testable against the other inventive parcels. Anicca is not imported as a source of EIC's terms. Anattā is not rewritten as a status dichotomy. The axiom is a claim about mixed physiology, not about the self. The handshake is real. The jobs remain different. A reader who wants the path will not find it here; a reader who wants a physiology of mixed systems will not find it in the Nikāyas.
