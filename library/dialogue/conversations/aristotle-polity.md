## They say

Aristotle's *Politics* treats polity — a mix of oligarchic and democratic elements, leaning toward the middle — as a constitution more durable than the pure types, which degenerate. The many and the few both have a claim; a city that is only one of them is already on the way to faction. The mixed constitution is not a mush. It is a design that refuses to let one part be the whole.

A reader who has Madison in one hand already has Aristotle in the other, whether or not the lineage is named.

## Conciliatorics' view

Mixed Synthesis names two interstitial states of a mixed physiology: unification (lasting by gathering authority) and conciliation (staying functional by accepting that a form will end). Aristotle's mix is nearby as a constitutional fact: a city that is not only one social part. It is not yet the physiological claim. A mixed constitution can still unify — a durable settlement that will not be revised. It can also conciliate — offices that rotate, claims that are allowed to end.

## Where they meet

Both refuse purity as a political good. Both treat a one-part city as a danger. Lijphart's consociationalism, Aristotle's polity, and Madison's auxiliary precautions are not one theory. They are a family of mixings. A reader of government systems expects this plate.

## Where they part

Aristotle is classifying constitutions and looking for durability of a *polis*. Conciliatorics treats durability as the pole that has to be argued for, not assumed as the good. "This mix has lasted" can be viability. It can also be unification. The *Politics* is not a Dex family. The handshake is the mix. The jobs remain different.
