## They say

Ludwig von Bertalanffy's *General <plain>System</plain> Theory* (1968) is the book most readers will have heard of when someone says "systems theory." Bertalanffy was not adding another special science. He was naming what the special sciences were already repeating: organization, wholeness, open forms that exchange with their surroundings — a pattern that showed up in biology, then in cybernetics, then wherever people were tired of taking the world to pieces and never putting it back.

The hope in that project is a general theory. If the same form can be recognized in an organism, a machine, and a society, then a shared vocabulary might let a biologist and an engineer stop talking past each other. The risk, which the popular afterlife of the book has not always treated as a risk, is that the general theory becomes the one theory: a unification of systems work under a single science.

## Conciliatorics' view

The treatise opens by assuming a meta-discipline, Systemics, and then stepping around the debate about whether such a thing must exist. What it will not step around is a second claim: Systemics should not seek or force a total integration of concepts from each analytical approach. It is an upper bound and a container, not a solvent.

Conciliatorics sits inside that container. It is one perspective, with its own starting model — Behavior Dynamics — and its own job: reconciling the inventive side of intervening. Other starting models can be compatible. They are not required to dissolve into this one. Cybernetics, ecology, and mathematics remain neighbors.

## Where they meet

Both treat systems as a subject in their own right, not only as a metaphor that mathematics, biology, or sociology may borrow when it is convenient. Both are impatient with a heap of disconnected special sciences that never speak. Anyone who already lives in more than one field will recognize the appetite.

## Where they part

GST, in the form most people meet it, leans toward unification. Conciliatorics leans toward coexistence. A term from another systems tradition is welcome when it does not hinder the work. It is not rewritten until it belongs to EIC. The world's <plain>interactions</plain> are already dense. Flattening the vocabulary so that it appears simpler than the situations it is meant to hold is not a gift to the reader.
