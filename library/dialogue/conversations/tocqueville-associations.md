## They say

Alexis de Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (1835–1840) treated the art of associating as democracy's school. Equality of conditions can flatten into a soft despotism: each withdrawn, the center mild and vast. Voluntary associations — townships, churches, newspapers, projects — teach people to act together without waiting for the state. A reader who has only "tyranny of the majority" already has the other half: the majority is less dangerous where associating is a habit.

## Conciliatorics' view

Status Reciprocity reads conciliation as decentralization in ordinary names. Operation's <plain>community</plain> is receptivity with permeability. Tocqueville's associations are nearby as a civic fact: many centers of action, none of them the only center, learned as a craft.

EIC does not take up equality of conditions as a historical thesis, and it does not rewrite the township as a Dex id. The contact is the art, not the prophecy about America.

## Where they meet

Both treat a lonely center plus withdrawn individuals as a failure of holding. Ostrom's polycentricity, Putnam's thinning associations, Tocqueville's art, and Status Reciprocity can sit together. A reader of government systems expects this plate.

## Where they part

Tocqueville is writing a political sociology of democracy, with a fear of democratic despotism. Conciliatorics is not a theory of equality, and it does not take up mores as his problem. "Association" is not Community in the compass's technical sense — the word collides; the jobs need not. The handshake is many centers, learned. The jobs remain different.
