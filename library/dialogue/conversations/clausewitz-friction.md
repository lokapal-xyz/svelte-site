## They say

Carl von Clausewitz's *On War* (1832) made friction famous: everything that distinguishes real war from war on paper — weather, fear, incomplete reports, the mud. Genius works through friction; it does not abolish it. A reader who has only "war is the continuation of policy" already has the other half: the continuation does not travel cleanly.

## Conciliatorics' view

Friction, in Resolution, is structural — a load that does not sit — paired with <plain>conflict</plain>, which is behavioral. Clausewitz's friction is the world's resistance to a plan. The word is the same. The job is not.

A challenge that ignores friction in EIC's sense dismisses a load. A plan that ignores Clausewitz's friction pretends the map is the march. Both are failures of taking the situation as it is. They are not one concept.

## Where they meet

Both refuse paper war, paper policy, paper systems. Scott's high modernism, Meadows' wrong leverage, and Clausewitz's mud rhyme. A reader of strategy and of government expects this plate, if only because the treatise chose the same word.

## Where they part

Clausewitz is writing a theory of war. Conciliatorics is not. His friction is not concatenated with chaos and <plain>order</plain> to yield potential and stability. Importing *On War* as Resolution would be a master tongue. The handshake is the word, honestly split. The jobs remain different. When the treatise says friction, it does not mean the mud of Jena.
