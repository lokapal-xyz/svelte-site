# Representation Types

*On different eyes.*

Traditional Philosophy has one native tongue: the written word. Arguments are built, defended, and transmitted in prose, and prose alone is expected to carry the full weight of a concept, however intricate. We have already started to leave that habit. What remains is to name the departure, and to add a fourth type that the operators did not yet need.

A concept as self-referential and densely interconnected as Attributes Dynamics resists being fully captured by any single <plain>medium</plain>. Prose is excellent at nuance, argument, and context, but it struggles to hold many simultaneous relationships in view at once — by the time a sentence explains how a fourth term relates to the first three, the reader has often lost the shape of the whole. A symbol can hold that shape in a glance, but it says nothing about *why* the relationship holds. A diagram can show structure instantly, but it cannot argue. None of this implies that one <plain>medium</plain> is superior to the others. A diagram is not a better tool than an argument. It is a different tool.

We therefore commit to four distinct **representation types**, each covering ground the others cannot:

- **DISCURSIVE REPRESENTATION**: Representation through written or spoken language. The traditional <plain>medium</plain> of philosophical discourse, and the one carrying argument, nuance, and context — everything that requires connective reasoning to be understood.

- **SYMBOLIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through compact notation — the operators, letters, and equations introduced throughout Attributes Dynamics. It exists to hold dense relationships in a single, precise, and reusable form that prose would need paragraphs to restate each time.

- **GRAPHIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through diagrams and images. It exploits spatial and visual relationships — position, proximity, symmetry — to make a structure legible at a glance, in a way that sequential language cannot.

- **SCHEMATIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through structured, field-based records — content organized into explicit, consistent formats rather than free-flowing prose. Its principal value is one the other three representations do not offer: **storage**.

The operator table is already symbolic. The <plain>primary jurisdiction</plain> diagram is graphic. This paragraph is discursive. None of those four is optional decoration on top of the "real" discursive content. Let's focus for a moment on the last representation type, as it is the most unusual of the set.

## Schematic Representation

A discursive case study must be written anew each time, at real cost in effort and length. A diagram can show one instance clearly but not a hundred at once. A schematic record, by contrast, can be filled in quickly, compared against others of its kind, and accumulated into a genuine library — of definitions, of symbols, of worked examples — that stays organized and queryable as it grows. This is less a way of *explaining* a concept than a way of *keeping* one, and everything that has been derived or applied from it, without the collection collapsing into disorder as it expands. It also happens to be a form unusually legible to AI systems, a consideration we will return to eventually.

Here is a small excerpt, so the shape is visible rather than only described. We will go deeper later in the treatise.

```
operators:

  - id: symmetry
    family: operators
    symbol: "≡"
    notes: >
      A status qualifier inside a compound equation — for example the (b ≡ B)
      clause inside Functionality. It never produces a new named concept as the
      outermost operator.

  - id: asymmetry
    family: operators
    symbol: "≢"
    notes: >
      A status qualifier inside a compound equation — for example the (b ≢ B)
      clause inside Dysfunctionality. It never produces a new named concept as
      the outermost operator.
```

Where earlier chapters have already used symbolic and graphic representation alongside prose, later chapters will begin using schematic representation as well — most notably as a way of recording and accumulating applied case studies, a use we will return to in Attributes Synthesis.
