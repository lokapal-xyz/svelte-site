## Representation Types

*On different eyes.*

Traditional Philosophy has one native tongue: the written word. Arguments are built, defended, and transmitted in prose, and prose alone is expected to carry the full weight of a concept, however intricate. We already departed from that norm when discussing Attribute Operators, where we noted that discursive, graphic, and symbolic representation would all be used together, so that the weaknesses of one could be compensated by the others. We want to make that departure explicit and complete it, because it deserves to be treated as a foundational choice rather than a stylistic habit.

The reasoning is simple. A concept as self-referential and densely interconnected as Attributes Dynamics resists being fully captured by any single medium. Prose is excellent at nuance, argument, and context, but it struggles to hold many simultaneous relationships in view at once—by the time a sentence explains how a fourth term relates to the first three, the reader has often lost the shape of the whole. A symbol can hold that shape in a glance, but it says nothing about *why* the relationship holds. A diagram can show structure instantly, but it cannot argue. And people, too, differ in which of these they metabolize fastest—some readers only feel a concept click into place once they see it drawn, others once they see it compressed into an equation. None of this implies that one medium is superior to the others. A diagram is not a better tool than an argument; it is simply a different tool, useful to a different eye. We therefore commit to four distinct **representation types**, each covering ground the others cannot:

- **DISCURSIVE REPRESENTATION**: Representation through written or spoken language. The traditional medium of philosophical discourse, and the one carrying argument, nuance, and context—everything that requires connective reasoning to be understood.

- **SYMBOLIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through compact notation—the operators, letters, and equations introduced throughout Attributes Dynamics. It exists to hold dense relationships in a single, precise, and reusable form that prose would need paragraphs to restate each time.

- **GRAPHIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through diagrams and images. It exploits spatial and visual relationships—position, proximity, symmetry—to make a structure legible at a glance, in a way that sequential language cannot.

- **SCHEMATIC REPRESENTATION**: Representation through structured, field-based records—content organized into explicit, consistent formats rather than free-flowing prose. Its principal value is one the other three representations do not offer: **storage**. A discursive case study must be written anew each time, at real cost in effort and length; a diagram can show one instance clearly but not a hundred at once. A schematic record, by contrast, can be filled in quickly, compared against others of its kind, and accumulated into a genuine library—of definitions, of symbols, of worked examples—that stays organized and queryable as it grows. This is less a way of *explaining* a concept than a way of *keeping* one, and everything that has been derived or applied from it, without the collection collapsing into disorder as it expands. It also happens to be a form unusually legible to AI systems, a consideration we will return to eventually.

These four types are not redundant with each other, and none is optional decoration on top of the "real" discursive content. Where earlier chapters have already used symbolic and graphic representation alongside prose, later chapters will begin using schematic representation as well—most notably as a way of recording and accumulating applied case studies, a use we will return to in Attributes Synthesis.
